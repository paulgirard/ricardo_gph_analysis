###############

This paper makes two important contributions. First, the authors have made an admirable effort in building a new dataset of bilateral trade reaching back to the 1830s, including colonies and other dependent territories, a considerable achievement in itself. Second, they bring network-science methods of community detection into the long-run analysis of trade blocks, a field so far dominated by methods from trade economics, and apply them over almost two centuries. I have three brief comments.

Trade block measures. (1) AN is not a network measure but a fixed, pre-defined map based on geography and the authors' judgement, one that partly reflects the world around 1990. I found it most useful as a benchmark, for instance on slide 49, where it shows how far actual trade blocks deviate from a geographical yardstick. It would help to make this benchmark role explicit. (2) Louvain does not give a unique solution. You mentioned running it many times: it would be useful to show the spread across runs, for example as bands around the curves, and to discuss countries or regions that are not consistently attached to one block. These "ambiguous" countries may be historically meaningful in their own right.

Modularity versus trade intensity. Slide 49 shows that in the interwar period blocks traded more among themselves than their size predicts, which is the familiar story of protectionism and Imperial Preference. Yet slide 50 shows modularity falling after 1918: blocks persisted but became less sharply separated. I find this an interesting and novel result. How do you interpret it? One possibility is weighting: Trade intensity bias (TIBI) is dominated by large economies, while modularity weights all countries equally. And since modularity depends on network size, is the population of entities stable enough, given the many new states created after 1918?

Correlates of trade blocks. The main results, a negative effect of distance and a positive effect of empire that weakens with decolonization, are familiar from gravity models of bilateral trade. The alliance effect appearing only after 1945 seems more novel. What do you see as the specific benefit of explaining block membership rather than bilateral trade flows?
